## Artefact 1: YouTube Video

### *AI Ethics: What You Need to Know* (365 Data Science 2025)  

<img width="1280" height="720" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/9a7b531e-9450-4516-a6ad-ffe29f66ee85" />

**Link:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRDWcUClP_Y

**Summary of the artefact**

This 2025 video from 365 Data Science introduces the foundations of AI ethics, walking through why classical ethical theories — utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics — matter when building and deploying AI systems. It explains that ethical AI development means weighing outcomes (a consequentialist, utilitarian concern) against rules, duties, and consent (a deontological concern), and traces the widening gap between AI capability and AI regulation (365 Data Science 2025).

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I chose this video because it laid out the three classical ethical theories side by side before applying them to real AI dilemmas, matching exactly what our workshop's opening session covered. Seeing utilitarian and deontological reasoning applied to something concrete, like content moderation on a chatbot, made abstract philosophy feel immediately relevant to decisions I could face building AI tools myself.

## Artefact 2: News Article

### *Top AI Ethics and Policy Issues of 2025 and What to Expect in 2026* (AI Matters, Medsker & Scallan 2026)

**Link:** https://aihub.org/2026/03/04/top-ai-ethics-and-policy-issues-of-2025-and-what-to-expect-in-2026/

**Summary of the artefact**

Published by AIhub in March 2026, this article reviews the major AI ethics controversies of 2025, including copyright lawsuits over training data, AI-generated deepfakes, children's exposure to chatbots, and the environmental cost of large models (AI Matters, Medsker & Scallan 2026). It frames these as clashes between competing ethical claims: the utilitarian argument that AI's benefits outweigh its costs, against deontological concerns about consent, rights, and the duties companies owe to the people whose data and likeness they use.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I selected this article because it shows that ethical theory is not just an academic exercise — it shapes real lawsuits, regulation, and public backlash happening right now. It reinforced our workshop's point that the same AI decision can look justified under one ethical theory and wrong under another, depending on whether you weigh outcomes or duties.

## Artefact 3: Scholarly Article

### *Ethical Theories, Governance Models, and Strategic Frameworks for Responsible AI Adoption and Organizational Success* (Madanchian & Taherdoost 2025)

<img width="863" height="601" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/4d01e472-68fc-492b-8e06-c53e3edb1da5" />

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2025.1619029

**Summary of the artefact**

Published in 2025 in *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence*, this peer-reviewed review examines how utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics each inform different governance models for responsible AI adoption in organisations (Madanchian & Taherdoost 2025). It argues that no single theory is sufficient on its own; organisations instead blend consequentialist risk assessment with rule-based accountability structures and virtue-based cultures of responsibility to manage AI ethically.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

This source gave me the theoretical depth to understand why our workshop kept returning to the same three frameworks rather than settling on one "correct" theory. It showed me that applying ethical theory to AI is less about winning a philosophical debate and more about combining frameworks into a workable governance structure — directly useful for evaluating my own future AI projects responsibly.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Personal Reflection

### Weighing Ethical Frameworks in Practice

<!-- ![Workshop attendance](./your-image-filename.png) -->

During our ethics workshop, we worked through a case study on whether an AI moderation system should automatically remove borderline content or flag it for human review. Applying utilitarian reasoning suggested automatic removal, since it prevents the most harm to the most users, but a deontological lens raised concerns about due process and a user's right to be heard before their content is removed. Having already watched the 365 Data Science video, I recognised this tension immediately rather than treating it as a new problem. The AIhub article and the Madanchian and Taherdoost review reinforced that this same tension plays out at scale in real AI governance debates. This portfolio has shown me that no single ethical theory gives a complete answer on its own, and that recognising which framework is driving a decision is often more useful than searching for one "right" theory.
