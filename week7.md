### *Disney v. Midjourney: Who Owns AI Art?* (LegalEagle 2025)

**Link:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-AxM2-4so-Y

**Summary of the artefact**

In this 2025 video, lawyer and YouTuber Devin Stone (LegalEagle) breaks down the landmark copyright lawsuit filed by Disney, Universal, and DreamWorks against AI image generator Midjourney in June 2025 (LegalEagle 2025). He explains the legal theory behind the suit — both direct and secondary copyright infringement — and why using copyrighted characters such as Darth Vader and Shrek to train and generate images raises different legal questions than earlier "AI authorship" cases like Thaler v. Perlmutter.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I chose this video because it translated a dense 110-page lawsuit into a clear explanation of how copyright law actually works, tying the training-data question and the output question together in one case. It connected directly to our workshop's discussion of intellectual property as an economic incentive: without protection, Disney argued, there is little reason to keep investing in original characters.

## Artefact 2: News Article

### *In First-of-its-Kind Lawsuit, Hollywood Giants Sue AI Firm for Copyright Infringement* (Veltman 2025)

<img width="1109" height="874" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/12f9ed53-164d-453e-9fea-0a7023a35072" />

**Link:** https://www.npr.org/2025/06/12/nx-s1-5431684/ai-disney-universal-midjourney-copyright-infringement-lawsuit

**Summary of the artefact**

This June 2025 NPR article by Chloe Veltman reports on the first major Hollywood lawsuit against a generative AI company: Disney, Universal, and DreamWorks suing Midjourney for allegedly using their copyrighted characters, including Shrek, Homer Simpson, and Darth Vader, to train and generate images (Veltman 2025). The article situates the case alongside earlier suits by the *New York Times*, Sony Music, and Getty Images, showing a pattern of major rights holders challenging generative AI training practices.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I chose this article because it captures IP law in the middle of an unresolved, real-time dispute rather than as settled theory. It connects to our workshop's point that intellectual property exists to balance creators' rights against public access to information — a balance that generative AI is testing in ways copyright law was never designed for.

## Artefact 3: Scholarly Article

### *Copyright and AI Training Data — Transparency to the Rescue?* (Buick 2025)

<img width="1493" height="362" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f2caff05-8cc7-4363-96eb-ad2b4ccd1311" />

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1093/jiplp/jpae102

**Summary of the artefact**

Published in 2025 in the *Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice*, this peer-reviewed article by Adam Buick examines whether requiring AI companies to disclose their training data, as the EU AI Act now does, can resolve the copyright problems raised by generative AI (Buick 2025). It argues that transparency alone cannot fix the underlying legal uncertainty, since disclosure only reveals what was used, not whether that use was lawful under existing copyright exceptions such as fair use or text-and-data-mining rules.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

This source gave me the legal detail behind the headlines, explaining exactly why transparency requirements are not a complete solution. It reinforced the workshop's distinction between IP policy tools, like disclosure rules, and IP law itself, and helped me understand why the Disney v. Midjourney case still needed to go through the courts rather than being resolved by regulation alone.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Personal Reflection

### Copyright's Incentive Logic Meets Generative AI

<img width="960" height="1280" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8c3dbfdb-1815-4f18-b180-7ae52f83854a" />

During our IP workshop, we discussed the traditional justification for copyright: it exists to give creators a temporary monopoly so they have an incentive to keep producing original work. Applying that logic to generative AI raised an immediate tension in our group discussion — if an AI company can train a model on thousands of copyrighted characters without paying for them, then generate unlimited new images of those characters, the incentive structure copyright was built to protect starts to break down. Having already watched the LegalEagle video, I understood the legal mechanics of the Disney v. Midjourney case well enough to follow this discussion in real time. The NPR article and the Buick article showed me this is not a hypothetical problem being debated in a classroom; it is an active legal fight with billions of dollars and thousands of jobs riding on the outcome. This portfolio has made me think more carefully about how any AI tool I build, including an Uzbek-language NLP model, would need to consider what data it was trained on and who owns the rights to it.
