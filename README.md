## Artefact 1: YouTube Video

### *Large Language Models Explained Briefly* (Sanderson 2024)

<img width="1280" height="720" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/74794a8e-ce89-4fab-bea9-0fa1eedf9bf5" />

**Link:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LPZh9BOjkQs

**Summary of the artefact**

This 2024 animated video by Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown), made for an exhibit at the Computer History Museum, explains how large language models (LLMs) actually work. It shows that an LLM is a mathematical function that predicts the most probable next word in a sequence, using billions of parameters refined through pre-training on huge text datasets and adjusted with backpropagation, gradually turning random output into coherent, human-like language (Sanderson 2024).

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I chose this video because it removed the "black box" feeling I had about tools like ChatGPT, which came up in our workshop discussion of generative AI fundamentals. The visual explanation of transformers and parameter tuning gave me an intuitive foundation to build on, and it reinforced why large, high-quality training data matters — directly relevant to my interest in eventually building NLP tools for the Uzbek language.

## Artefact 2: News Article

### *Three Things to Help Improve Low-Resource Language AI, According to Stanford* (Albarino 2025)

<img width="1393" height="691" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6d49e986-6858-4b39-be03-c6464ad5a23f" />

**Link:** https://slator.com/three-things-to-help-improve-low-resource-language-ai-according-to-stanford/

**Summary of the artefact**

This 2025 Slator news article reports on a Stanford HAI white paper examining why LLMs consistently underperform for "low-resource" languages that lack sufficient digital text and labelled data (Albarino 2025). It outlines competing strategies — massively multilingual models, regional models, and monolingual fine-tuned models — plus methods for generating training data through machine translation, before recommending investment, participatory research, and equitable data ownership to close the global AI language gap.

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

I chose this article because it speaks directly to the "digital divide" affecting languages such as Uzbek, which have far less training data than English or Chinese. It connects to our workshop's discussion of data quality and bias in AI systems, and it confirmed that my long-term goal of building Uzbek-language NLP tools addresses a recognised, real-world gap rather than a niche problem.

## Artefact 3: Scholarly Article

### *Natural Language Processing Applications for Low-Resource Languages* (Pakray, Gelbukh & Bandyopadhyay 2025)

<img width="1334" height="365" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7f8b0f72-08f9-4a42-afb5-990640e60c42" />

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1017/nlp.2024.33

**Summary of the artefact**

Published in 2025 in the journal *Natural Language Processing*, this peer-reviewed survey reviews techniques developed for low-resource languages such as Bodo, Khasi, Mizo, and Assamese. It covers tasks including part-of-speech tagging, sentiment analysis, machine translation, and hate-speech detection, and highlights transfer learning and multilingual transformer models such as mBERT and XLM-R, alongside community-driven dataset creation, as key strategies for languages that lack large digital corpora (Pakray, Gelbukh & Bandyopadhyay 2025).

**Justification on why I chose the artefact**

This scholarly source gave me technical depth beyond the news article, showing concrete methods such as transfer learning from high-resource languages to build NLP tools with limited data. It extended the transformer concepts covered in our workshop and gave me a realistic starting point for an Uzbek NLP project of my own: fine-tuning existing pre-trained multilingual models rather than training something from scratch.
